If you have MS and search the internet for "MS diet," you'll be flooded with information — forums, books, videos, everyone with a different recommendation. The topic is hot in the MS community and it's important to approach it honestly.

## Let's clarify something from the start

No diet cures MS. There is no food that can stop the immune system from attacking myelin. If someone promises you otherwise — be skeptical.

What a diet can do is give your body better building materials — more beneficial nutrients, less inflammation, more energy. That's not trivial. But it's not a cure.

## Why different people say different things

You'll hear people who swear by a certain diet — "it changed my life, I feel like a new person." And you'll hear others who tried the same diet and felt no difference. Both groups are telling the truth — about themselves.

MS is different for everyone. Everyone's body reacts differently to changes in nutrition. A diet that helps one person may do nothing for another. This doesn't mean one of them is wrong — it means there's no universal answer.

## Three popular diets you'll hear about

**The Swank Diet** — created by Dr. Roy Swank in the 1940s. The core idea is to eat far less saturated fat — no more than 15 grams per day. It recommends more fruits, vegetables, and fish, less red meat and processed foods. Some studies show that people on this diet report less fatigue.

**The Wahls Protocol** — created by Dr. Terry Wahls, who herself lives with MS. The diet is similar to paleo eating — lots of vegetables (nine cups a day), lean meat and fish, no processed foods, no gluten, and no dairy. Dr. Wahls claims this diet significantly improved her own symptoms.

**OMS (Overcoming MS)** — a program that includes not just diet but a whole lifestyle approach. The dietary part resembles Swank — plant-based food, fish, no meat or dairy, with the addition of vitamin D and omega-3 supplements.

All three diets have something in common: more fruits and vegetables, fewer processed foods, less saturated fat. These are principles that are good for anyone, not just people with MS.

## What does science say

The honest answer: science still doesn't have a definitive conclusion. There are small studies showing promising results — reduced fatigue, better quality of life. But there's no large, convincing study that says "this diet helps with MS."

This doesn't mean diets are pointless. It means scientists don't yet have enough data to say with certainty which diet works and why.

## Vitamin D

You'll hear a lot about vitamin D and MS. Indeed, research shows that people with MS often have lower vitamin D levels. Whether low vitamin D contributes to the disease or is simply an accompanying factor — that's still unclear.

Many neurologists recommend keeping your vitamin D levels within the normal range. But don't start taking supplements on your own — get tested first and talk to your doctor about the right dosage.

## Practical advice

Instead of jumping into a radical diet tomorrow, think about simple changes:

- More vegetables and fruits on your plate
- Fewer processed foods and sugar
- More fish, less red meat
- Enough water
- Talk to your doctor before cutting out entire food groups

And remember: nutrition is important, but it's only one piece of the puzzle. Don't blame yourself if you don't follow a perfect diet. Nobody does. Also watch out for products and programmes that promise more than the science supports — read [how to spot scams](/en/scams).
