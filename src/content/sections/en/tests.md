## MRI (Magnetic Resonance Imaging)

This is the most important test in MS. You'll have it done not just at the beginning, but periodically to see how things stand.

**What it is**: You lie inside a machine that takes very detailed images of your brain and/or spinal cord. It doesn't hurt, there's no radiation, but it's noisy and takes about 30–60 minutes. If you don't like enclosed spaces, tell your doctor beforehand — there are ways to help.

**What doctors look for**: The images show small spots that doctors call "lesions" or "plaques." These are the places where myelin has been damaged. Think of them as traces the disease has left behind.

**What the results mean**: Your doctor will look at how many spots there are, where they are, and whether some of them are "new" or "active." Active spots mean there's recent inflammation. Old spots mean there was damage there before, but things are quiet now.

More spots don't necessarily mean more symptoms. Some people have many spots on their scan and few symptoms, while for others it's the opposite.

**Contrast dye — when and why**: Sometimes during an MRI you'll get an injection of contrast dye into your vein. This isn't done every time. The contrast helps doctors see whether a spot is "active" right now — meaning whether there's new inflammation happening at this moment. If the MRI is a routine check and the doctor just wants to compare images with previous ones, contrast may not be needed. If anything about the contrast concerns you — ask your doctor why they're ordering it and whether it's necessary in your case.

## Lumbar puncture

Not all people with MS go through this test, but your doctor may request it, especially at the beginning.

**What it is**: A thin needle is used to take a small amount of fluid from the lower part of your back. It sounds scary, but the procedure itself is usually quick and done with local anesthetic. You might have a headache afterwards — lying down and drinking plenty of water helps.

**Why it's done**: Doctors test the fluid for certain proteins called "oligoclonal bands." If they're present, it's a sign that the immune system is active in the nervous system. Their presence supports an MS diagnosis, but on its own it's not enough to confirm one.

## Blood tests

Blood tests in MS aren't done to confirm MS. They're done to rule out other conditions that can resemble MS. Your doctor wants to be sure that what you have is really MS and not something else.

## How often will you have tests

MRI is usually done once or twice a year, but it depends on your situation. Your doctor will tell you the schedule. These regular check-ups are important — they help catch changes early, even if you're feeling fine.

Don't be afraid of the tests. They're not a punishment — they're a way for you and your doctor to see what's happening inside, so you can make the right decisions together. Read also [how to work effectively with your medical team](/en/medical-team).
