## Why people with MS are targets

When you're told you have a chronic disease with no cure, it's completely normal to search for a way out. Anyone would. And that's exactly what scammers count on — your fear, your hope, and your desire for something to change.

They know you're ready to try almost anything. And they use that.

And MS helps them without them even knowing it. The disease is extremely unpredictable — people can go into remission for 5, 10, even more years. If someone takes a "miracle" supplement and happens to go into natural remission — they'll believe until the end that the supplement "cured" them. And they'll tell everyone who asks. But the remission would have happened without the supplement. That's not a cure — that's a coincidence.

## Red flags — how to spot them

If you hear or read something about MS and it contains even one of the following — be very cautious:

**"Complete cure"** — there is no cure for MS. Full stop. Anyone who claims otherwise is either lying or doesn't understand the disease.

**"Doctors are hiding the truth"** — doctors aren't hiding anything. Medical science is open — anyone can read the published research. If someone claims there's a secret cure that "the system is suppressing" — that's a classic manipulation.

**"100% natural, no side effects"** — any substance that has an effect on the body can also have a side effect. "Natural" doesn't mean "safe." Snake venom is natural too.

**"Thousands of people have already been cured"** — but there are never names, data, or verifiable evidence. Just anonymous "testimonials."

**"Limited time only" or "Special price"** — medicine doesn't work with promotions. If the cure is real, it won't expire tomorrow.

**Videos with dramatic transformations** — "I was in a wheelchair, now I'm running." No medical documentation, no control group, nothing verifiable. Just emotion.

## Popular scams you might encounter

**CCSVI (Chronic Cerebrospinal Venous Insufficiency)** — a theory that MS is caused by narrowed veins and that "unblocking" them would cure the disease. Serious scientific studies were conducted and the result was definitive: it doesn't work. Despite this, some clinics continue to offer the procedure — for thousands of euros.

**Bee therapy (apitherapy)** — bee stings with the idea that the venom has a healing effect on MS. There's no scientific evidence it works, and it carries real risks — including severe allergic reactions.

**"Secret" dietary supplements** — products sold online with promises of "myelin restoration" or "immune system reset." They're usually expensive, unregulated, and have no scientific evidence whatsoever.

**Stem cells in unregulated clinics** — don't confuse the legitimate HSCT procedure (which we discuss in the treatment section) with clinics, usually abroad, offering "stem cell therapy" without real medical oversight. They're dangerous, expensive, and have no proven effectiveness.

**Books and courses promising "the truth about MS"** — an entire industry of authors and "coaches" selling books, online courses, and programs with promises of revealing "what really causes MS" or "how to cure yourself." If someone had discovered a cure for MS, it would be published in medical literature and win a Nobel Prize — not sold as an e-book for 50 euros.

## How to verify information

Before believing something that sounds too good to be true, ask yourself these questions:

- Is there a published scientific study on this? Not a blog, not a video, not a Facebook post — a real study in a medical journal.
- Who is behind the claim? A doctor? A scientist? Or someone selling something?
- Does your neurologist recommend it?
- Does it mention risks and side effects, or only benefits?
- Are they asking you for money upfront?

If something doesn't pass these questions — it probably doesn't deserve your money, your time, or your hope.

## The financial and health cost

Scams aren't just "harmless." People pay thousands of euros for useless treatments. Worse — some stop their real therapy because they believe they've found something better. And lost time without effective treatment means more accumulated damage.

False hope is expensive — both financially and health-wise.

## One simple rule

If it sounds too good to be true — it almost certainly isn't true.

If someone promises you something that thousands of scientists worldwide haven't been able to achieve — ask yourself why this particular person succeeded and they didn't. The answer is usually simple: they didn't succeed. They're just lying.

Trust your neurologist, ask questions, and remember — current medicine may not be able to cure MS, but it can help you live well. That's not nothing.
