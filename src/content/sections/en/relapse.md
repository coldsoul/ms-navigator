## What is a relapse

A relapse is a period when new symptoms appear or existing symptoms get noticeably worse. This happens because the immune system has caused new damage to the myelin somewhere in the nervous system.

For it to be considered a relapse, symptoms usually need to last at least 24 hours and not be linked to another cause — such as a fever or infection.

A relapse can look very different from person to person. For one person it might be blurred vision in one eye. For another — numbness in the legs. For someone else — sudden fatigue or balance problems. There's no "typical" relapse — that's why it's so confusing at first.

## Relapse or just a bad day?

This is perhaps the hardest question you'll face. Not every worsening is a relapse.

There's something doctors call a "pseudo-relapse" or "pseudo-exacerbation." This is a temporary worsening of existing symptoms caused by something else — heat, stress, poor sleep, an infection, or even a hot shower. When the cause passes, the symptoms return to their usual level.

A simple check: if you're feeling bad but symptoms improve after rest, cooling down, or a good night's sleep — it's more likely a pseudo-relapse. If the symptoms are new or persist for days without improvement — it could be a real relapse.

But don't worry if you can't tell the difference. Even doctors sometimes can't say right away. Over time, you'll get to know your body better.

## What to do during a relapse

First and most important — don't panic. Relapses are part of the disease and don't mean something has gone fatally wrong.

Call your neurologist and describe what you're experiencing. Try to write down:
- what symptoms you have
- when they started
- whether they're getting worse or staying stable

This information will help your doctor assess what's happening and whether treatment is needed.

## When to call your doctor immediately

Some situations require quick action:
- sudden loss of vision in one or both eyes
- severe weakness in your limbs that prevents you from walking
- problems with speech or swallowing
- severe dizziness or double vision

In these cases, don't wait — contact your doctor or go to the emergency room.

## What happens at the hospital during a relapse

If your doctor determines you're having a relapse that needs treatment, the most common approach is a course of corticosteroids — usually administered intravenously over several days.

Corticosteroids don't "cure" the relapse — they reduce inflammation and help the body recover faster. Recovery can take weeks and sometimes months. Be patient with yourself.

## Between relapses

In the periods between relapses, you might feel completely normal, or you might have residual symptoms from previous relapses. Both are normal.

The most important thing you can do between relapses is follow the treatment your doctor has prescribed and take care of yourself — sleep, movement, less stress. You can't control whether or when the next relapse will come, but you can give your body the best conditions to cope.
