Let's be honest from the very start: as of today, there is no medication that can cure MS completely. That's hard to hear, but it's important to know so you don't fall for promises that don't match reality.

The good news is that there are therapies that can significantly slow the disease and reduce relapses. And they're getting better every year.

## What is the goal of treatment

Treatment for MS isn't like an antibiotic for an infection — you don't take it and the disease goes away. The goal of modern therapies is different:

- To reduce the frequency and severity of relapses
- To slow the accumulation of new damage in the nervous system
- To slow the progression of the disease

In short — treatment doesn't "fix" MS, but it helps your body gain time and space to cope better.

## Types of treatment

### Relapse treatment

When you have an active relapse, your doctor may prescribe a course of corticosteroids — usually administered intravenously over several days. They reduce inflammation and help the body recover faster. They don't treat the disease itself — they help specifically with the relapse.

### Disease Modifying Therapies (DMTs)

This is the main category of long-term treatment for MS. DMT stands for Disease Modifying Therapy — a therapy that changes the course of the disease.

There are many different DMTs — some are taken as pills, others as injections, and some are given as infusions in hospital. They differ in effectiveness, mechanism of action, side effects, and the risks they carry. More effective therapies usually come with more serious potential risks — that's why the choice is always a balance between benefit and risk.

We won't list specific medications here because:
- The list changes — new therapies are approved regularly
- Not every medication is suitable for everyone
- The choice depends on your type of MS, disease activity, and many other factors

One of the most important things to understand about MS treatment: the therapy is as unique as the disease itself. A medication that works brilliantly for one person may not work for another. That's not failure — that's how MS works.

Even doctors can't say with full certainty whether a particular therapy will help. Sometimes trial and error is necessary — especially with more aggressive forms of the disease, medications may be changed several times until the one that works for you is found. If your treatment is changed — don't panic. It doesn't mean something has gone wrong. It means your doctor is searching for the best option for you.

This is a conversation you need to have with your neurologist. They know your case and can explain which options are suitable for you, what risks each one carries, and why they're recommending a particular therapy.

### HSCT (Hematopoietic Stem Cell Transplantation)

HSCT is a more aggressive approach in which the immune system is "rebooted." The procedure uses chemotherapy to destroy the existing immune system, then rebuilds it using stem cells.

This is not suitable for everyone. HSCT is considered mainly for people with aggressive relapsing-remitting MS where standard therapies haven't worked. The procedure carries serious risks and requires an extended hospital stay.

HSCT is not a "cure for MS." In a successful procedure, the goal is to stop the immune attack, but damage already done remains. If you're considering HSCT — talk to your neurologist in detail about the risks and benefits in your specific case.

## Symptom management

Separate from therapies aimed at slowing the disease, there are also medications and approaches for managing specific [symptoms](/en/symptoms) — fatigue, spasticity, pain, bladder problems, and others. You don't have to just endure it. If a symptom is interfering with your daily life — tell your doctor. There are ways to help.

## Your right to participate in the decision

MS treatment isn't something that just happens to you. You have the right to understand what's being proposed, why, and what the alternatives are. You have the right to ask questions, seek a second opinion, and actively participate in choosing your therapy.

If something isn't clear — ask. If you're not happy with your treatment — speak up. You know your body best. Read more about [how to work effectively with your medical team](/en/medical-team).
